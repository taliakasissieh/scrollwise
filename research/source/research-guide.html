Research Guide

Does an intentional stopping point change how teenagers browse social media?

For the ScrollWise student team · Version 1 · November 2026
Use with ScrollWise research mode (Settings → About and privacy → Research mode)

What we compare

Mode A: a conventional feed that keeps loading.
Mode B: the ScrollWise feed with limited posts and a stopping point.

Design

Every participant tries both feeds. Half do A first, half do B first (counterbalancing).

Who

Volunteer students aged 13–17 at your school, with school approval and parental consent where required. Aim for 20–40.

Time

About 15 minutes per participant, including two feeds of up to 5 minutes each and short questions.

What we record

Browsing time, posts viewed, how each feed ended, choices at the stopping point and short neutral questions. Anonymous codes only.

What we report

Honest descriptive results with their limits. A small study gives early signals, not proof.

Before anything else

Show this guide to a teacher and get your school’s approval before you invite anyone. Follow your school’s rules about parental consent. Taking part must always be voluntary.

1Research question and hypothesis

Research question. Does an intentional stopping point (Mode B) change how long teenagers browse a social media feed, how many posts they view and how in control they feel, compared with a conventional continuously loading feed (Mode A)?

Hypothesis (H1). With the stopping point (Mode B), participants will browse for less time and view fewer posts than with the continuous feed (Mode A), and will rate stopping as easier and feel more in control.

Null hypothesis (H0). There is no difference between Mode A and Mode B in browsing time, posts viewed, ease of stopping or feeling of control.

A hypothesis is a prediction to test, not something to prove. Your job is to collect fair data and report what it shows, even if it doesn’t support the hypothesis.

2Variables

Independent variable (what we change)

The type of feed: Mode A (continuous feed that keeps loading posts) or Mode B (ScrollWise feed that stops after a set number of posts or the time the participant chose, then offers “I’m done” or “Continue intentionally”).

Dependent variables (what we measure)

Controlled variables (what we keep the same)

  • The same posts and post design in both modes. ScrollWise shuffles the posts once per participant and uses that order for both feeds (the second feed starts halfway through it).
  • The same maximum time for both feeds (we recommend 5 minutes) and the same Mode B post limit (we recommend 8) for the whole study.
  • Comments and profiles are switched off in both modes.
  • The same device (or the same model), brightness and volume, with notifications turned off.
  • The same quiet room and seating, and a similar time of day where possible.
  • The same spoken instructions (use the script in section 7) and no hints about which feed is “better”.

3Participants and recruitment

  • Who: students aged 13–17 at your school who use social media, recruited with a teacher’s permission.
  • How many: at least 20, ideally 30–40, with an equal number doing A first and B first. Because everyone tries both feeds, each person gives you a direct A-versus-B comparison.
  • How to invite: a short announcement in class or a poster, approved by your teacher. Say what’s involved (about 15 minutes, browsing a sample feed, a few questions), that it’s anonymous and that it’s completely voluntary.
  • Fair mix: try to include different classes and year groups, not only your friends, so your sample isn’t one small group.
  • Who shouldn’t take part: members of the ScrollWise team and anyone who knows the hypothesis, because they might act differently.
  • No pressure: don’t offer prizes or marks that would make people feel they have to take part.

Realistic schedule: with two devices and two researchers per device, you can test about 8 people in a 45-minute lunch break. Three or four sessions are enough for 30 participants.

4Consent, privacy and safety

The rules we always follow

  • School approval first, and parental consent wherever your school requires it (often for students under 16).
  • Voluntary: anyone can say no, stop during the test or leave without giving a reason. Tell them this before they start.
  • Anonymous: use codes (P001, P002…). Never write a name, email, phone number, class or age into ScrollWise or onto the recording sheet.
  • Only what we need: research mode records timings, counts and the optional answers. Nothing else.
  • Safe content: the feed shows fictional sample posts suitable for teenagers. If someone feels uncomfortable, stop.
  • Supervision: run sessions with a teacher or another responsible adult nearby.

Withdrawing later

Don’t keep a list that links codes to names. If someone might want to withdraw their data later, give them a slip with their code. If they bring it back, delete their rows from your spreadsheet (and in research mode, if the data is still on the device).

Looking after the data

  • Export the CSV after each session and save it in a school account (for example your school’s OneDrive or Google Drive), not on a personal phone.
  • After checking the file opens, use Delete all results in research mode so the device holds no data.
  • Only share summaries (averages, charts). Don’t publish individual comments that could identify someone.
  • Delete the raw files when the project ends, unless your teacher asks you to keep them.

5Equipment and set-up

You need

  • One or two phones or tablets (ideally the same model), charged, with internet.
  • “Do Not Disturb” on, brightness fixed, sound off.
  • The ScrollWise website open in the browser.
  • Printed: this guide’s script, the order list and the recording sheet (appendix).
  • A quiet space where the participant can browse without being watched closely.

Research mode settings

  1. Open Settings → About and privacy → Research mode.
  2. Maximum time per feed: 5 min.
  3. Posts before the stopping point: 8.
  4. Order: Alternate (recommended), or set by hand from the order list when you use more than one device.
  5. Keep these settings the same for the whole study.

Run one practice session with a team member first, then delete that practice record (use the bin icon next to it) so it doesn’t end up in your results.

6Counterbalancing

People often behave differently in the second feed, simply because it’s second: they may be more bored, more relaxed or more familiar with the posts. If everyone did Mode A first, any difference could come from the order, not from the stopping point.

The fix: half the participants do A then B and half do B then A. Order effects then affect both modes equally.

  • One device: leave Order on Alternate. ScrollWise gives the 1st participant A→B, the 2nd B→A, and so on.
  • Two or more devices: each device alternates on its own, which can make the groups uneven. Use the printed order list (appendix) and choose “A then B” or “B then A” by hand for each code.
  • Check: the results overview shows how many participants did A→B and B→A. Keep them equal.
  • If a participant has to stop after the first feed, you can finish the missing feed later: enter the same code and ScrollWise runs only the missing feed, in the same order.

7Step-by-step procedure

About 15 minutes per participant. One researcher runs the session; another can welcome the next person.

  1. Welcome and explain. Read the script below. Check they’re happy to take part and remind them they can stop at any time.
  2. Set up. In research mode, check the code (ScrollWise suggests the next one, e.g. P007) and the order. Tick the confirmations and tap Start. Write the code and order on the recording sheet.
  3. Hand over the device. The participant reads “Before you start” and taps Continue.
  4. Feed 1. They browse as they normally would. Stay quiet, don’t watch over their shoulder and don’t comment. In Mode B they first choose how long they’d like to browse. The feed ends when they tap “I’m done” or at the maximum time.
  5. Questions about feed 1. They answer or skip. ScrollWise saves the record as soon as they tap Next.
  6. Feed 2 and its questions, the same way.
  7. Final question. One question about both feeds and an optional comment. Then the thank-you screen.
  8. Hand back and note. Take the device back and tap “Researcher: back to setup”. On the recording sheet, note anything unusual (an interruption, a question they asked), using the code only.

Script to read out

“Thanks for helping with our project. You’ll try two versions of a sample social media feed, one after the other. The posts and people are made up. Please browse the way you normally would, for as long as you like. You can stop whenever you want by tapping ‘I’m done’, and each feed also ends by itself after a few minutes. After each feed there are a few short questions. There are no right or wrong answers, and nothing is linked to your name. You can stop taking part at any time. Do you have any questions?”

Don’t explain the hypothesis or say which feed is ScrollWise’s until after the participant has finished. If they ask, say you can explain afterwards.

8Collecting and exporting data

Each feed is saved in the browser as soon as its questions are answered or skipped. The data stays on that device until you export it. At the end of every session:

  1. Open research mode and scroll to Results on this device.
  2. Export the data:
    • Computer or Android: tap Download CSV. The file scrollwise-research-YYYY-MM-DD.csv goes to your Downloads folder.
    • iPhone or iPad: tap Share CSV (if shown) and choose Save to Files or your school email. Download CSV also works and saves to Files → Downloads.
    • If neither works: tap Copy CSV and paste into a note or spreadsheet, or tap Show CSV text and copy it by hand.
  3. Open the file in Excel or Google Sheets (Google Sheets: File → Import → Upload) and check the rows are there.
  4. Save it to your school account, then tap Delete all results on the device.
  5. Combining several devices or days: copy the rows (without the header) from each file under one header row. Every row has a unique record_id, so you can spot accidental duplicates.

An empty file with the right columns is included as research/sample-structure.csv. It contains no participant data.

10Analysis and suggested charts

Step 1: clean the data

  • Delete practice rows and rows from anyone who withdrew.
  • For the main comparison, keep participants with both feeds (two rows with the same code). Also report how many were left out and why.

Step 2: describe each mode

WhatGoogle Sheets / Excel formula (data rows 2–200)
Average browsing time, Mode A (minutes)=AVERAGEIFS(J2:J200, D2:D200, "A")/60
Median browsing time, Mode B (minutes)=MEDIAN(FILTER(J2:J200, D2:D200="B"))/60 (Google Sheets, Excel 365)
Average posts viewed, Mode A=AVERAGEIFS(K2:K200, D2:D200, "A")
% who stopped by choice, Mode A=COUNTIFS(D2:D200,"A",M2:M200,"yes")/COUNTIF(D2:D200,"A")
% who continued at the stopping point (B)=COUNTIFS(D2:D200,"B",P2:P200,"yes")/COUNTIF(D2:D200,"B")
Average “ease of stopping”, Mode B=AVERAGEIFS(V2:V200, D2:D200, "B") (blank answers are ignored)

Step 3: compare each person with themselves

Make a small table with one row per participant: their Mode A time, their Mode B time and the difference (B − A). Count how many browsed less in Mode B, and work out the average difference. This paired comparison is the strongest part of your design. You can also compare the A→B and B→A groups to check for order effects, and work out each person’s time-guess error (their guess minus their real time).

Optional, if your maths teacher can help: a paired t-test or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test tells you how likely a difference this big would be by chance. Descriptive results are fine on their own if you explain them carefully.

Step 4: suggested charts

1. Average browsing time by modeBar chart, one bar per mode. Add each person as a dot if your tool allows.
2. Each participant, A to BA “slope chart”: one line per person from their A time to their B time.
3. How the feeds endedStacked bars: stopped by choice vs reached the maximum time, for each mode.
4. Questions, side by sideGrouped bars of the average 1–5 answer for each question, Mode A next to Mode B.

These sketches only show the chart type: they contain no data. Use your own results, label the axes and write the number of participants (n) under every chart.

11Interpreting the findings honestly

  • Describe, don’t over-claim. Write “In our sample of [number] students, participants browsed for an average of [time] less with the stopping point”, filled in with your real results, not “ScrollWise is proven to reduce screen time”.
  • Report everything, including results that don’t support the hypothesis, and how many people took part.
  • Mode B is limited by design. Part of a shorter time in Mode B comes from the limit itself. The more interesting questions are whether people stopped at the stopping point or chose to continue, and how in control they felt.
  • The maximum time is a ceiling. If many people reached 5 minutes in Mode A, the real difference could be bigger than your data shows. Say how many reached the maximum.
  • Small differences may be chance. With a small group, a difference of a few seconds means very little.
  • Look for order effects. If the second feed is always shorter, mention it.
  • People act differently when observed, and may give the answers they think you want. Neutral questions and a quiet setting help, but can’t remove this completely.

12Limitations

  • A short supervised test isn’t the same as everyday use at home, over weeks.
  • The feed contains fictional sample posts, not participants’ own friends and interests, so it may be less engaging than a real app.
  • The sample is small and from one school, so results may not apply to all teenagers.
  • Mode A repeats posts once it has shown all the sample posts. With a small sample feed this happens sooner.
  • Answers are self-reported and can be affected by mood, the setting or wanting to help the team.
  • Results recorded on different devices can’t be compared if the settings were different. Keep them the same.

A good conclusion sounds like this

“Our small study suggests that… These results are an early signal, not proof, because… Next time we would…”
